Lecture 1 (Möller, 16/04/2026). Establishes the vocabulary: what a project is, what makes international construction projects distinctive, where the personnel comes from, how you organise yourself — and who exactly is sitting at the table.

What is a project? Four definitions

DIN 69901 — "Projects are undertakings that can be delimited in terms of content, planning and time and for which a separate, temporary organization is created."
  • forms a unique project due to novel and complex tasks
  • is limited in time by start and end
  • has limited human, material and financial resources (budget)
  • has a project-specific organization (team)

The magic triangle

SCOPE (features, functionality, performance) — COST (resources, budget) — TIME (schedule, production rate), with QUALITY at the centre. Project management wraps around it: communication, target planning, organization, documentation, cost planning, coordination.

Process vs. project

"A process is a structure whose elements are tasks, task owners, material resources and information linked by logical sequential relationships. A process has a defined start event (input) and result (output) and serves to create value for customers." A project runs from initiation through implementation to completion, containing many such input–process–output chains.

The control loop: project planning (structure, dates, resources, quality, budget) → plan → execution → as-is → project controlling → deviation → measures/change → back into planning. Lessons learned feed back to the target; milestones and feedback set the rhythm.

International construction projects — why they fail

The course works with prominent negative examples. They are easy to remember and cover the typical risk categories.

Case study: A2 Express Highway, Poland (COVEC)

49 km of highway from the German border to Warsaw, to be completed before the 2012 European football championship, ~€420m, awarded to COVEC (China Overseas Engineering Group) — the first Chinese entry into Europe's construction market. Government estimates put construction costs at ~€1bn; rivals complained about price dumping. Contract terminated June 2011, dispute not settled until 2017.

Reasons for project failure — a compact list of the classic mistakes in foreign construction:
  • Chinese contractor not used to European standards
  • failed to provide financial records
  • poorly organized leadership
  • underestimated costs of Polish wages and materials
  • Polish subcontractors refused cooperation and to provide materials
  • Poland believed the Chinese government would subsidise losses if necessary
  • communication gap due to cultural differences

Case study: Mtentu Bridge, South Africa (STRABAG)

Africa's highest bridge, ~€130m, 1,132 m long, 30 m wide, 223 m high, JV STRABAG–Aveng, construction period planned Nov 17 – Feb 20. In February 2019 the JV terminated the contract given the risk to the safety and wellbeing of its personnel: protests by villagers angry at broken promises of employment, workers and management threatened — including with assault rifles. In August 2023 the new contract went to a CCCC/MECSA JV, at twice the original price, completion end of 2027.

Counter-example: San Giorgio Bridge, Genoa — the "Genova Model"

After the Ponte Morandi collapse (14 August 2018, 43 killed, 600 left homeless), the new bridge was completed in 15 months, ~€200m, 1,100 m long, 30 m wide, 56 m high, built 24 hours a day, 7 days a week without stopping.

"Genova Model" — a new approach:
  • Collaboration — working together towards the same objective
  • Transparency — opened to the public
  • Teamwork — throughout the whole supply chain

Background: UK construction productivity has barely moved in 50 years (1970–2020) — "nearby no increase in productivity in the construction industry over the last 50 years."

Approaches to staffing

The three staffing strategies — a classic exam topic, because they contrast so cleanly.

Ethnocentric approach

also: monoculture, home country orientation

  • employees from the home country
  • project management techniques of the home country
  • usually no consideration of foreign specifics
  • home country remains the main market

Polycentric approach

also: multiculture, host country orientation

  • host country as the focus of corporate efforts
  • goal: highest possible integration into the foreign market
  • high autonomy of the foreign organization
  • if necessary, development of an own host country image

Geocentric approach

also: mixed culture, world market orientation

  • goal: world market position
  • alignment of the company's activities with the global market
  • globalized corporate decisions

Forms of organization of international projects

  1. Influence organization — project manager acts as mediator between the specialist departments, has no direct authority to issue directives or make decisions, works closely with management. ➢ rather in small projects.
  2. Matrix organization — superimposition of two equivalent lines of instruction; each employee reports to the department head and the project manager. ➢ frequently used when all organizational areas of the company are affected.
  3. Autonomous organization — exclusive responsibility of the PM; all authority to issue directives and make decisions lies with the PM; employees are outsourced from various business units; high flexibility. ➢ very widespread in the international project business.

Fields of activity of German foreign construction

The lecture distinguishes four service constellations, each drawn across national borders and legal systems: national (everything in Germany) — traditional I "classic case" (German contractor, client in country X; special case development aid with a Development Aid Bank in country Z) — traditional II "international investor" (site in country X, client in country Y) — traditional III (German employer with an international field of activity) — area business (branch in the host country, profit transfer to Germany).

Stakeholders in foreign construction

Project participants (stakeholders) — a person, group of people or organization
  1. that is actively involved in the project or is influenced by the course or outcome of the project, and
  2. which, if applicable, can influence the course of the project or the project result.
"(to) have a stake in sth. = einen Anteil an etwas haben" — split into internal and external stakeholders, each with their own setting (goal, interests).

Classic national base structure (Germany)

Client — contract¹ → designer / consulting engineer, who handles approval and monitoring towards the public authorities and holds potential supervision and instruction authority over the contractor. Between client and contractor sits the construction contract².

¹ Architect or engineer contract ("planner contract") — must be adapted to the specific project so that it regulates the powers of the "owner's construction manager" and specifies which powers of instruction the owner's representatives have towards the contractor. ² Construction contract according to BGB and/or VOB.

International structure according to FIDIC

The FIDIC contract corresponds to the Anglo-Saxon point of view. Its special feature is the special position of the "Engineer":
  1. competent representative of the employer ("building owner")
  2. organization and control of technical, legal and economic issues
Participants in the full structure: Financier (financing institutions such as the World Bank or KfW — cash flow) · Employer (with Employer's representative, an employee empowered on a case-by-case basis) · Engineer (with Engineer's representative and a Resident Engineer on site) · Consultants (specialist engineers commissioned by the Engineer: geotechnical engineer, quantity surveyor …) · Contractor (with Contractor's Representative) · Subcontractor (selected by the contractor) and nominated subcontractor (selected by employer and/or engineer).
