Risk — possibility of loss, harm, or an undesired outcome resulting from uncertainty. Four risk groups in "traditional" construction work abroad and their hedging instruments:

The six risk categories in detail — each in addition to the "usual" construction risks:
  • Economical — unpredictability of the market, fluctuating exchange rates, high inflation, funding, foreign exchange restrictions, supply embargos
  • Political — unstable political conditions, revolutions/wars
  • Legal — short-term changes to laws/regulations, legal differences
  • Technical — different standards, technical differences, different technological requirements
  • Cultural — different understanding of PM (and PM tools), habits of communication and collaboration
  • Geographical — climatic uncertainties, geological uncertainties
Plus business management (corporate marketing) as a cross-cutting category. Concrete manifestations shown in the lecture: strike, civil war, extreme weather conditions, accidents, contractual risk.

A cautionary image: the excavation collapse in April 2007 during the digging of a 20-metre-deep pit for the 73-storey Infinity Tower (now Cayan Tower) in Dubai — "Project risks should never be underestimated, as they can threaten the very existence of a construction company!"

  6. high investment

The central formula:

Risk = Impact (of an event) × Probability of occurrence (of the event)

Put into practice in the Risk & Opportunities Register: for each item it records the description, remarks, allocation (client / contractor), type (cost / time / contractual / technical), the amount affected, the share affected in %, the amount in € and the probability in % — from which the risk value (−) or (+) follows.

Example from the slide: item 2.1.1 "Geology and hydrology — infiltration, injection and drainage"; additional measures due to leakages in the retaining wall; amount affected €1,000,000, 100 % affected, probability 50 % → risk value €500,000.

Degree of influenceability — why early decides everything

The curve of cost potential influence falls steeply while investment costs rise. Distributed across the HOAI work phases, the potential is roughly:
  • ~35 % in optimization phase 1 — assessing customer wishes (project development, work phases 0–1)
  • ~38 % in optimization phase 2 — assessing solutions (optimization during planning, work phases 2–4)
  • ~22 % in optimization phase 3 — tendering, pre-qualification of executing companies, negotiations, contracts (work phases 5–7)
Risks must be identified as early as possible so that appropriate countermeasures can be taken as soon as possible.

The 9 HOAI work phases: 1 basic evaluation · 2 preliminary planning · 3 design planning · 4 approval planning · 5 implementation/construction planning · 6 preparation of tendering · 7 participation in awarding contracts · 8 construction supervision / site management · 9 project supervision and documentation.

Never forget the human factor! — Möller illustrates how badly people judge probabilities using the German "6 aus 49" lottery: the chance of the jackpot is 1 in 139,838,160. For comparison, the probability of being struck by lightning during a thunderstorm is ~1 in 3 million. The probability of winning nothing on a single bet is over 98 % — the payout rate is only ~50 %.

Monitoring the risks:
  • change of risks in the different phases of a project
  • project decisions lead to the emergence of new risk situations — their new individual risks must be identified, analyzed and managed
  • risks that have not been assessed and classified as serious can suddenly become a threat to success
Monitoring the measures:
  • periodic monitoring of the effectiveness of treatment measures
  • adaptation of coping measures in case of changes in risk potentials
  • example: does adjusting construction sequencing and procedures mitigate schedule risks as identified?

Principles of risk-conscious action

Ten sentences — short enough to memorise:
  1. We only accept controllable risks.
  2. We do not deviate from established processes.
  3. We are committed to the highest safety standards.
  4. We only work within the scope of our competence.
  5. We only accept orders of appropriate size.
  6. We ensure personnel competence and capacity.
  7. We work only with efficient and reliable partners.
  8. We only work under contract.
  9. We do not allow ourselves to be blackmailed.
  10. We report risk-relevant events without delay.

1 · What is Technical Controlling all about?

TC is the systematic planning, monitoring and control of technical project parameters (scope, quantities, construction processes) to ensure economic efficiency and achievement of project objectives — in short: expressing all project-relevant technical processes in monetary terms.

Core areas of responsibility: performance and quantity controlling · construction process and schedule controlling · cost control (technical perspective) · risk monitoring · change order management (claims, variations / compensation events).

Objectives: early identification of deviations · transparency within the construction project · ensuring economic efficiency · supporting site management in decision-making.

Internationally these tasks are typically spread across several roles — cost engineer, quantity surveyor, project controls manager and claims consultant — which in the German construction industry often merge into a single function: the technical controller. Characteristics: interdisciplinary working, communication bridge, supporting holistic decision-making.

"Technical Controlling is not about reporting numbers. It is about understanding what is happening in the project, identifying deviations early, and actively steering the outcome."

2 · Estimation — the backbone of the project

Estimation is the backbone that connects all project phases: it links tendering, contracting and execution through consistent estimating principles.

Three estimates across the project life cycle — one continuous solution with the same data management, continuously updated:
  1. Tender estimate (pre-construction: project initiation, tender preparation, tender submission) — strategic and uncertainty-driven; pricing and risk-positioning
  2. Contract estimate (contract: negotiation, award) — the contractual reference point; defines scope, cost and obligations
  3. Work estimate (delivery: mobilization, project execution, project closeout) — operational planning and steering tool; continuously updated with real data
ESTIMATION = reference system · TECHNICAL CONTROLLING = steering mechanism.
"Without a solid understanding of the estimate: no meaningful analysis, no informed decisions."

3 · Project steering — bridging plan and reality

"You don't manage the past — you steer the future by continuously controlling the expected outcome."

The steering loop: set reference point with your estimate → check reality → analyze deviations → steer your project → check and balance your estimate → back to the reference point.

What impacts the project margin?

A typical margin trajectory across a project: at contract negotiation, shifting of risk and revised payment terms · M1 mobilisation cost and site installation reviewed · M3 productivity below plan, material cost increases · M6 mitigation measures · M12 approved change orders · close-out final cost/revenue adjustments, residual risks materializing.

Practical steering instruments

  1. Plan vs actual comparison — cost (planned vs actual), productivity (planned vs actual output), progress (planned vs achieved). The key follow-up question for any deviation: single event or trend?
  2. Structured change order management — not all revenue is equal; the level of certainty must be transparent! The activation of revenue must be subject to clear regulations and principles.
  3. Risk assessment — aligned with reality
Claim levels and their typical mistakes:

Level 3 — technically valid, formally submitted, acknowledged by the client. Critical: negotiation power is unclear, contractual interpretations may differ, outcomes are often reduced amounts, delays and partial approvals.
Typical practical mistake: often treated as "almost secured revenue" and assigned overly high probabilities → systematic overestimation of margin and distorted project forecasts. Level 3 becomes critical when many claims remain stuck at this stage, conversion to level 4 is slow, and negotiations drag on over time.

Level 4 — formally rejected by the client, and we do not accept the rejection; potential escalation (claim, dispute, arbitration, litigation). Critical: no secured revenue, but also not definitely lost; typically involves long resolution time, high effort, uncertain success probability.
Typical practical mistake: treated internally as "probable revenue" or kept "in the pipeline" → significant distortion of the true project picture.
Disputed claims are not neutral — they represent high-risk expectations, not reliable revenue.

What distorts a clear project picture — seven traps

  1. Optimism bias / wishful thinking — overconfidence in productivity assumptions, underestimation of risks, a "we will catch up later" mindset, ignoring early negative trends → problems are recognised too late.
  2. Misclassifying facts as risks — known issues reported as "risks" instead of being treated as current problems (the cost impact has already been incurred) → delays recognition of the real impact; false sense of control.
  3. Overstating revenue (claims and variations) — recognition of unapproved change orders as secured revenue, optimistic assumptions on claim success, missing link between contractual position and actual enforceability → artificially inflated margin.
  4. Underreporting costs — delayed recognition of cost overruns, "smoothing" cost curves, deferring bad news → margin appears stable, then collapses suddenly.
  5. Snapshot thinking (instead of trends) — focus on a single reporting date, ignoring development over time → early warning signals are missed.
  6. Incomplete scope visibility — missing quantities, hidden scope growth, interfaces not fully captured → systematic underestimate of total cost.
  7. Lack of integration (silos) — cost, schedule and risk are not aligned, and different "versions of truth" exist → wrong conclusions despite correct data.

Key takeaways of the guest lecture

  • Estimation is the backbone — it defines the reference point across all project phases.
  • Project steering bridges plan and reality — by continuously monitoring and adjusting performance.
  • Margin is not fixed — it is dynamic — influenced by cost, revenue and risk events.
  • Early warning is critical — project failures are not sudden, they develop over time.
  • Transparency can be distorted — data must be interpreted critically, not blindly trusted.
Technical Controlling in one sentence:
Plan the work. Measure reality. Detect deviations. Take action.
