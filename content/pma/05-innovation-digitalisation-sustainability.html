The core claim: an idea is not enough

"It is not enough to have a brilliant idea; it must also be implemented!" — two pieces of evidence from the history of technology:
  • Electric cars: around 1900 the range of electric vehicles was around 100 kilometres. In the USA, 40 % of cars were steam-powered, 38 % were electric, and only 22 % ran on gasoline. The technology existed — it still did not win.
  • 3D printing in construction: in 1939, William E. Urschel created the world's first 3D concrete printed building in Valparaiso, Indiana. The machine automated the mixing, pouring and shaping of concrete. — "3D printing has not just been invented!"

An example of successful scaling: AutoCAD

  • 1982 AutoCAD; it ran on PCs (MS-DOS) — unlike the expensive workstations that other CAD systems required
  • 1980s: made CAD software affordable for smaller offices and engineers
  • 1990s: became the industry standard for architecture, mechanical engineering and construction (the DWG standard)
  • 1994: 750 AutoCAD training centers
  • Success factors: relatively inexpensive, modular and expandable, compatible

The construction industry's share of global resource consumption — the four figures to know:
  • RESOURCE-INTENSIVE — the construction industry accounts for around 50 % of global material requirements
  • ENERGY-INTENSIVE — the construction and real estate industry is responsible for around 40 % of global energy demand
  • HIGH EMISSION — the construction and real estate industry accounts for around 40 % of global CO₂ emissions
  • WASTE-PRODUCING — around 50 % of the world's waste is generated by the construction industry
In Lecture 1 the lecturer gives the figure 38 % of global carbon emissions (cradle to grave) as the core of the "lack of sustainability" problem.

The construction industry's four biggest challenges (ICoM slide from Lecture 1): lack of sustainability (38 % of global carbon emissions) · lack of efficiency (low productivity and no disruptive innovation) · lack of commitments (exploding costs and delays) · lack of qualified personnel (33.5 % of companies on German building construction sites reported problems finding qualified personnel — ifo Institut, 2022). The slide's conclusion: "Consistent digitalization is without alternative."

Life cycle cost

The initial and follow-up costs of a building stand in a ratio that makes the initial investment look small — which is why looking at the whole life cycle (rather than just construction costs) is the basis of any sustainability assessment.

Definition of climate neutrality (United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change): the endeavour to minimize greenhouse gas emissions as much as possible and to offset unavoidable emissions through systematic offsetting measures.

How it is meant to work — four levers

The first three levers avoid CO₂, the fourth compensates for what remains:
  1. Resource-efficient & long-lasting construction — CO₂-reduced buildings, circular buildings, building in existing buildings
  2. Ecological & climate-friendly building — climate-neutral production, use of renewable raw materials, transformation of energy
  3. Efficient construction & operation — digitization, efficient construction methods (e.g. prefabrication and LEAN), use of renewable energies
  4. Intelligent compensation — CO₂ certificates, carbon capture

Eight action areas for energy consumption

  1. Buildings — climate-neutral operation of administration buildings (own and third-party) used by the Group
  2. Passenger cars / commercial vehicles — conversion of the vehicle fleet to renewable energy sources
  3. Construction site power — electrification and environmental optimisation of small equipment, office containers and cranes
  4. Construction machinery / heavy goods vehicles — conversion to renewable energy sources
  5. Asphalt mixing plants — conversion to renewable energy sources
  6. Stone and gravel plants — conversion to renewable energy sources
  7. Concrete plants / other production — conversion to renewable energy sources
  8. Efficiency increase — potential to increase energy efficiency through conversion to new technologies such as electrification

For each action area, key actions linked to targets for 2030 and 2040 have been defined, along with specific metrics to help monitor implementation and effectiveness, monitoring and reporting, and an annual target/actual comparison of the metric.

Circular economy

Slide 20Slide 21Slide 24Slide 25

The high demand for raw materials in construction and the large amounts of waste generated through demolition make the building industry one of the most resource-intensive sectors of the economy. Due to the finite availability of resources, the linear economic system — raw material extraction, use and disposal — is increasingly reaching its limits.

Goals of a circular economy:
  • consider buildings over their entire life cycle: saving raw materials, increasing efficiency, making greater use of secondary or renewable raw materials, and avoiding waste
  • develop end-to-end material and waste concepts and introduce a waste management system that also includes the handling of hazardous waste
  • continuously increase the percentage of recycled materials for construction and investigate the use of alternative, renewable building materials
  • during the design and build phases, consider not only the origin of the materials used but also their possible uses at the end of their service life: materials and components should be removable, separable and reusable or recyclable

These developments also offer opportunities: the reuse and recycling of raw materials not only reduces costs in procurement and disposal but also opens up new business areas — for example in the production and use of sustainable building materials and the renovation of existing buildings. Methodologically the lecture works with the 9R framework of the circular economy and with material efficiency strategies across the product life cycle (UNEP).

PCF — Product Carbon Footprint: the sum of greenhouse gases emitted and removed in a product system, expressed in CO₂e, for the entire life cycle of a product.

Energy background

Europe 2022/23 scenario: > 24 % fossil fuels, 24 % nuclear, 17 % hydro, 13 % solar, 17 % wind. Target scenario 2050 (best case): carbon neutrality — and for that, storage of renewable energy is key.

Dam construction and hydropower — the key facts

  • carbon neutrality can only be guaranteed if the installed hydropower capacity doubles
  • there are approximately 9,000 dams worldwide — only 25 % for electricity generation
  • construction surged in the 1950s and peaked in the 1970s → the available timeframe is now 25 years, versus 75
  • this does not yet consider sedimentation or the decommissioning of aging dams
  • the best locations are already taken → alternative locations imply severe hazards (environmental impact, climate change)
→ The lecture's answer to "Best solution?": Pumped Storage Hydro (PSH).

Reference example Goldisthal (Germany, 2003): Germany's largest pumped storage hydro plant at 1,060 MW, delivering full power for up to 8 hours. For scale: 1 MW ≈ 750 to 1,000 homes.
