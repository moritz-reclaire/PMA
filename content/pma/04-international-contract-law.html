Lecture 4 (Möller, 18/06/2026) with the extensive guest lecture by Robert Murgatroyd (Senior Contract Manager, STRABAG Contract Management Legal). The largest contract chapter of the course — and the one with the most examinable terminology.

The guiding idea of the guest lecture: read FIDIC as a project-management system, not as a list of clauses. Five questions you should be able to answer afterwards:
  1. Why international contracts? — a shared system for cross-border projects
  2. Who decides? — roles and authority: Employer, Contractor and Engineer
  3. Who owns risk? — the FIDIC books allocate design, site, time, price and operation risk
  4. What if reality changes? — variations, delay, payment and claims handle uncertainty
  5. How should PMs behave? — ask early, document clearly, notify on time and keep decisions traceable

FIDIC = Fédération Internationale des Ingénieurs-Conseils (International Federation of Consulting Engineers).
  • international standards organization for construction technology and consulting engineering
  • founded 1913 in Ghent by Belgium, France and Switzerland; headquarters in Geneva
  • first standard form in 1957
  • first edition of the Rainbow Suite in 1999; second edition in 2017 (the 1999 editions remain valid)
  • represents nearly 1 million professionals in 75 countries
Groups of member associations: GAMA (Africa), ASPAC (Asia-Pacific); regional federations FEPAC (Panamerican) and EFCA (European).

Why international standard forms at all?

Six reasons from the guest lecture — the answer to "can we not just use the local contracts?":

  1. Consistency across borders — a well established and recognised framework parties can agree on
  2. Balanced risk allocation — reflects best industry practice, is perceived as neutral and balanced, building trust between the parties
  3. Neutral governing law — international contracts allow for a neutral governing law and dispute resolution (arbitration in a third country)
  4. Investor and lender confidence — the World Bank and development banks use FIDIC as a standard form
  5. Dispute resolution mechanisms — use of early warnings and risk registers
  6. Modular and flexible for different types of contracts and projects
"The contract is not paperwork after award; it is the project's governance system."
  • Common language — a standard form gives parties from different legal and commercial backgrounds a starting point they can all recognise
  • Balanced expectations — it allocates typical project risks in a way that is predictable enough for tendering and financing
  • Decision process — it gives procedures for notices, instructions, payment, claims and disputes when the project changes

FIDIC is not a law

Three common misunderstandings (Murgatroyd):
  • "FIDIC is a law!" → FIDIC books are standard forms operating under a specific legal system (New York Convention). They are neither a legal system nor laws. FIDIC contracts bind the parties, but legal provisions may change the interpretation given to the provisions of the contract.
  • "FIDIC is the same as the VOB — just in English!" → FIDIC books cannot be compared to the VOB as they are not embedded in a specific legal jurisdiction.
  • "The FIDIC books are the whole contract!" → they are the General Conditions of Contract, not the whole contract.
Practical implication: always ask both — "what does the contract say?" and "what does the governing law require?" Mandatory law, permits, safety rules and public policy can override the contract.

And: a contract is never hermetic. It cannot predict everything (complex projects generate surprises in scope, time, cost, interfaces and stakeholder behaviour), it creates defaults (definitions, procedures and roles reduce ambiguity when people disagree), and it relies on management — good contract administration is a project-management capability, not only a legal task. The better question is therefore not "where is the perfect clause?" but "what process helps the parties make a fair decision?"

FIDIC values: quality services · value for the client · cost efficiency · impartiality · high standards · performance · know how · integrity · sustainability · long-term perspective · respect for the environment.

What the contract aims to be — five properties:
  • Clear, understandable — necessary, essential clauses; detailed definitions of terms; consistent structure
  • Fair, equal — distribution of risk to the party that can bear it, control it and manage it
  • Third parties — designed and set up by independent consulting engineers
  • Complete, adaptable — scope meets normal needs, easily adaptable to specific requirements
  • Acknowledged — convincing FIDIC reputation, worldwide acceptance, tested and proven

The FIDIC Contracts Guide (2001) is the official explanatory document — a practical handbook to the Red, Yellow and Silver Books. It is not a contract, but a commentary and interpretation tool.

The Blue, Pink, Gold and Emerald Books are described in the lecture and in the FIDIC overview table rather than on a slide of their own.

The four main books in detail

Red Book — Construction
  • for building and civil engineering works designed by (or on behalf of) the Employer; the contract may require the Contractor to supply plant and/or execute some design
  • the contract comes into effect when the Employer accepts the tender by issuing the Letter of Acceptance; the parties may then sign a Contract Agreement
  • administration by the Engineer, with two options in the Appendix to Tender: (a) the Engineer acts impartially when carrying out certain actions, including making pre-arbitral decisions; or (b) the Engineer does not act impartially, and pre-arbitral decisions are made by the jointly appointed DAB
  • the Engineer certifies interim and final payments, determined by measurement, applying the rates and prices in the Bill of Quantities
Yellow Book — Plant and Design-Build
  • for plant and for building and engineering works designed by the Contractor; however, the Employer may have executed some design
  • coming into effect and Engineer options as in the Red Book
  • the Engineer certifies interim payments, which may be specified in the Schedules of Payments, and certifies final payment based on the lump sum contract price
Silver Book — EPC/Turnkey
  • for EPC (Engineer, Procure, Construct) contracts, where responsibility for the design lies solely with the Contractor in accordance with the Employer's Requirements
  • the contract typically comes into effect when signing the Contract Agreement
  • the Contractor carries out all engineering, procurement and construction, providing a fully equipped facility, ready for operation "at the turn of a key"
  • administration by the Employer — unless he appoints a Representative. There is no Engineer.
  • lump sum contract price; interim and final payments as work proceeds, usually against a Schedule of Payments
  • testing procedures including Tests after Completion
  • all claims from either party have to follow a strict procedure: the Employer must consult with the Contractor to reach agreement; if no agreement, the Employer makes a fair determination; if not accepted, the DAB takes over
  • the Contractor takes the majority of risks, and the Employer pays more to cover such risks — in exchange, final price and time of completion should be more certain
Green Book — Short Form
  • engineering and building work of relatively small capital value (guide: under US$500,000 or of limited duration)
  • suitable for fairly simple or repetitive work without the need for specialist sub-contracts
  • comes into effect upon receipt of the Agreement by the Contractor (after the Employer signs the acceptance of the offer/tender)
  • no reference to "the Engineer" — the Employer must nominate a spokesman or may appoint a representative
  • no advance payment; interim payments in accordance with the value of works executed; retention percentage stated in the Appendix
  • resolution of disputes: provision for amicable settlement, local adjudication, arbitration

Blue Book — dredging and reclamation work and ancillary construction, applicable to any size and complexity. Coming into effect as with the Green Book; the Employer must nominate an authorised person, administration by the Engineer. The works shall be designed by the Employer or his Engineer, but the form is also suitable to include, or wholly comprise, contractor-designed works. Advance payment provision and interim payments; the dredging retention is released in full on completion; if of long duration, there is an option to add for escalation. No remedying of dredging defects after completion — instead a "fitness for purpose" obligation. Resolution of disputes: amicable settlement, DAB (one or three members), ICC arbitration (three members).

Which contract to use?

The FIDIC decision tree: is it a straightforward project (fixed price — lump sum, little employer involvement, no major unforeseen risks)? If not: does the Employer design? If not: does the Contractor design, and is there plant and/or high unforeseen risk? If nothing fits: discuss the Employer's requirements in detail with the Contractor, and negotiate a modified FIDIC contract.

Golden Principles

Slide 42

Guest lecture 4 · International Contract Law (Murgatroyd)

Slide 27Slide 28

Introduced in 2019 because of much misuse of the FIDIC brand — contracts carried the FIDIC name while their risk allocation had been changed beyond recognition. The slide's motto: "Do not judge a book by its cover."

The five Golden Principles:
  1. Keep duties and obligations consistent with the chosen book.
  2. Draft Particular Conditions clearly and unambiguously.
  3. Do not change risk allocation so far that the form becomes misleading.
  4. Preserve reasonable time periods for notices and procedures.
  5. Keep dispute avoidance and resolution mechanisms workable.
The purpose behind them: protect balance (the forms are designed around an intended allocation of roles, risks and procedures) · control amendments (Particular Conditions can tailor the deal, but excessive changes may undermine the model) · support trust (PMs should see the principles as a warning against changing clauses without understanding the system).

Typical order of precedence:
  1. A) Contract Agreement — a short document stating the parties to the contract, the formal conclusion of the contract itself; lists the contract documents and sometimes the order of precedence, and sometimes the contract price
  2. B) Letter of Acceptance — the document making the contract "active"
  3. C) Letter of Tender — a simple document stating the sum of the offer and that the offer is binding until a stated date
  4. D) Particular Conditions Part A — Contract Data — a short, mostly tabulated document stating the main facts: time for completion, delay damages rate and cap, duration of the defects notification period, choice of law, choice of language, dispute resolution mechanisms
  5. E) Particular Conditions Part B — Special Provisions — contains all additions, deletions and/or changes to the General Conditions. Attention: may alter the initial contract significantly!
  6. F) General Conditions — the actual FIDIC books
  7. G) Specification (Employer's Requirements or Technical Specifications) — standard local norms such as VOB/C, BS, EN; these can also have "special specifications" should the Employer want to alter requirements
  8. H) Drawings — what is to be built (new in FIDIC 2017 by being explicitly mentioned)
  9. I) Schedules — schedules of payments, execution programme, schedules of guarantees, and possibly the BoQ
  10. J) Joint Venture undertaking (if any)
  11. K) Any other documents forming part of the contract
Möller's slide additionally lists the DAB agreement and warranties and guarantees (bonds/guarantees) as contract components.

Performance obligations and risks

Main performance obligations:

  • the Contractor shall plan the works to the extent specified in the contract (e.g. design, approval planning, alterations, execution/assembly/workshop drawings, formwork and reinforcement plans, fire protection plans etc.) — § 4.1, 4.11
  • the Contractor shall execute and complete the work — § 4.1, 4.11
  • the Contractor shall remove all defects that have occurred — § 4.1, 4.11

Ancillary performance obligations: the Contractor's duty of care towards personnel and work (§ 6) · type and manner of installations, materials and technical execution (§ 7) · observance of deadlines with commencement, delay and interruption (§ 8), implied terms (§ 4.10, 4.11, 5.1).

FIDIC basically distinguishes between two types of risk:
  • Risks of physical loss or damage
  • Risks of financial or time loss
It specifies and defines these risks and assigns them to the parties in §§ 17, 18 and 19 — in the logical sequence
Risk → Responsibility → Liability → Indemnity → Insurance.

The allocation follows the pattern: Employer's risks are specified exhaustively, Contractor's risks are all other.

Applicable law

In English law a contract is considered a "legally binding agreement" — it has the force of law, so to speak, and must be strictly observed. It is therefore essential to specify the applicable law (law of collision / construction site law) as the statute of the contract. According to FIDIC, the contract is governed by the law of the country specified in the Special Conditions.

The FIDIC contract therefore cannot regulate conclusively: the statute of limitations (within / after the defects notification period) and public law — construction site safety regulations, occupational health and safety / environmental protection regulations, and customs and foreign exchange regulations.

Definitions create a common language. "Contract", "Day", "Cost", "Works" and "Completion" are not casual words. Defined terms reduce ambiguity and make decisions auditable.

Example question: if the contract gives durations in days — calendar days or working days? → 1.1.3.9: "day means a calendar day and year means 365 days."

PM skill: read definitions before arguing about obligations. Typical trap: assuming a word has its everyday meaning. Useful habit: quote the defined term exactly in notices and records.
Communications create traceability. A notice only helps if it is sent through the contractually valid channel, to the right person, at the right time.

1.3 [Communication]: for approvals, certificates, consents, determinations, notices and requests, the communications shall be in writing and delivered by hand (against receipt), sent by mail or courier, or transmitted using any of the agreed systems of electronic transmission as stated in the Appendix to Tender.

→ Whether an email is a valid means of communication therefore depends on what the Appendix to Tender says. With electronic communication, a clear participation list needs to be established.

PM skill: set up the communication matrix before work starts. Typical trap: treating an informal letter, email or site chat as a formal notice. Useful habit: keep a decision log and evidence trail.

Clauses 2–5 — the parties

The contractual relationship is a governance triangle:
  • Employer — defines need, pays, accepts
  • Contractor — delivers and manages execution
  • Engineer — administers, certifies, determines
Between Employer and Contractor sits the FIDIC contract form; between Employer and Engineer a professional appointment contract; between Engineer and Contractor there is no direct contractual relationship. The Engineer administers the contract on behalf of the Employer.

The Engineer

The Engineer …

  • has the obligation to act fair when making determinations or certifying amounts for payments
  • is the "broker" between Employer and Contractor
  • is comparable to an Employer's Representative
  • is paid by the Employer and thus not completely impartial in reality

Main tasks — nine verbs worth memorising:

Limits of the Engineer's duty and authority:
  • the Engineer has no authority to amend the contract
  • the Engineer has no authority to relieve either party of any duties, obligations or responsibilities under the contract
  • the Engineer is required to obtain the approval of the Employer before exercising a specified authority; the requirements shall be as stated in the Particular Conditions
  • the Engineer needs specific approval of the Employer before:
    • agreeing on extension of time and/or additional cost
    • instructing a variation — except in emergencies, and if the variation would increase the Accepted Contract Amount by less than 1 %
    • approving a proposal for variation by the Contractor
    • specifying the amount payable in each of the applicable currencies

3.5 Instructions of the Engineer: the Engineer may issue to the Contractor at any time instructions and additional or modified drawings which may be necessary for the execution of the works and the remedying of any defects. The Contractor shall only take instructions from the Engineer to whom the appropriate authority has been delegated. If the instruction constitutes a variation, Clause 13 applies.

3.7 Determinations: several clauses in the FIDIC books allow the Engineer to make determinations (e.g. Clause 14.5 "Plant and Materials intended for the Works" for the cost of the plant and material). To make a determination, the Engineer shall consult with each party in an endeavour to reach agreement. If agreement is not achieved, the Engineer shall make a fair determination in accordance with the contract, taking due regard of all relevant circumstances.

Subclause 8.4 [Extension of Time for Completion] gives the overview of causes that may lead to an extension of time — but the specific details for the different causes are found in different clauses. Typical causes: variation, any cause of delay, exceptional weather, unforeseen shortages, delay by the Employer.

The pattern across the books is a decreasing ladder of entitlements: Red Book → Yellow Book (less) → Silver Book (even less) → Gold Book (the least). The more risk you take on, the less entitlement to extension of time you have.

Clause 13 — Variations and Adjustments

Show source slides

Guest lecture 4 · International Contract Law (Murgatroyd)

Slide 64Slide 65Slide 66

Projects change. The contract's job is to separate authorised change from unmanaged drift.

The variation process in four steps — it protects both sides because it creates a record of what changed, why it changed, who instructed it and how it affects price and programme:
  1. Trigger — need for change appears
  2. Instruction / proposal — authority and scope are clarified
  3. Assessment — time, cost and risk impacts are evaluated
  4. Record — the decision is documented and implemented

Change of law — the management lesson: the key idea is fairness against the tender baseline. The parties priced the project under one legal environment, and the contract may adjust if that environment changes. Three questions: baseline — what law and conditions were assumed at tender? impact — what cost, time or method change did the new requirement cause? record — can the team show the link between the legal change and the project consequence?

Claims are not automatically adversarial. They are the contract's mechanism for preserving rights and turning project events into assessable decisions. Three elements:
  • Entitlement — which contract event gives time or money relief?
  • Notice — was the other party informed in the required form and time? Otherwise the party loses entitlement — time bar
  • Substantiation — can cause, effect and amount be shown with records?
For substantiation, SMART applies: specific · measurable · achievable · relevant · time bound.

The escalation ladder

"Do not memorise the days first. Remember the ladder: notify, substantiate, consult, determine, dispute board, settlement, arbitration."

"Use it or lose it" — FIDIC claims are like rugby rucks: the right only survives if you play it in time.
  1. Act early — notices are mandatory, not optional
  2. Act continuously — claims must be managed during the project
  3. Act within time — late = no entitlement

The PM behaviour checklist

Show source slides

Guest lecture 4 · International Contract Law (Murgatroyd)

Slide 8Slide 77
The guest lecture's final synthesis — from contract clauses to project habits:
  1. Know roles — clarify who instructs, certifies, determines and decides disputes. Use the contract route.
  2. Notify early — send notices promptly, to the right party and in the right form. Protect against time bars.
  3. Keep records — capture dates, instructions, progress, resources, costs and correspondence as events unfold.
  4. Manage change — connect scope, programme, price and risk. Treat variations, claims and payment as one system.
  5. Escalate properly — consult and determine first; then move to DAAB, settlement or arbitration with evidence.
Habit to remember: "If it changes the work, record it; if it affects time or money, notify it; if it remains unresolved, escalate it."

"The Hidden Half of Project Management"

The frame Murgatroyd puts around the whole chapter: "As a PM you are not managing a contract — you are managing people, expectations and risk through it."

  • Contracts do not solve problems — people do. A perfect contract does not prevent a poorly managed project.
  • Complexity comes from stakeholders. Alignment can be much harder than understanding the contract.
  • The PM as translator. You manage expectations — not just tasks.
  • Most problems are behavioural, not technical. Issues escalate when communication breaks down.
  • Effective problem-solving is proactive. The best claims are the ones that never arise.

Historical opening of the guest lecture: the Code of Hammurabi (1755–1750 BC), written "to prevent the strong from oppressing the weak". The laws are casuistic, expressed as "if … then" conditional sentences — including the "eye for an eye" principle.
